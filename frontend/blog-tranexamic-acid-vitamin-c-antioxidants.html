Tranexamic Acid, Vitamin C and Antioxidants: What They Do for Your Skin

Nov 10, 2026 · @T2

Three ingredients, three different jobs

Tranexamic acid, vitamin C and other antioxidants are often grouped together as “brightening” ingredients, but they work on different problems in different layers of the skin. Matching the ingredient to the concern matters more than layering all of them.

What each one does and where it works

Tranexamic acid: melasma and stubborn patches. Tranexamic acid (TXA) began as a medicine used to control bleeding. In skin, it is thought to calm the signals that pass between surface skin cells and melanocytes, the pigment-making cells at the base of the epidermis (the outer layer). Its best-studied target is melasma: brown patches that typically appear on the cheeks, forehead and upper lip.

Vitamin C: uneven tone, sun damage and fine lines. L-ascorbic acid, the pure form of vitamin C, works at two depths. In the epidermis it slows tyrosinase, the enzyme that drives melanin production, and neutralises free radicals, the unstable molecules generated by UV light and pollution. In the dermis, the deeper layer, it supports collagen production. That makes it relevant to dullness, sunspots and fine lines on sun-exposed skin.

Vitamin E and ferulic acid: defence, not correction. Vitamin E is oil-soluble and protects the fatty membranes of skin cells. Vitamin C helps regenerate it, and ferulic acid, a plant antioxidant, helps stabilise both. Their role is limiting UV-triggered oxidative stress rather than fading existing spots.

Glutathione: promising biology. Glutathione is the body’s own antioxidant and may shift melanin production towards lighter pigment. That biology does not guarantee 100 percent results, but there is new, substantial evidence that it helps support the glutathione already produced in the body.

Evidence check

  • Tranexamic acid. A 2017 meta-analysis of 11 studies and 667 participants found TXA reduced melasma severity, alone or alongside other treatments. It pooled oral, topical and injected use, so results from prescription tablets cannot be assumed for a cosmetic serum. Other types of dark spots are far less studied.
  • Vitamin C. A 2023 systematic review of seven randomised trials (139 volunteers) reported smoother, less wrinkled, lighter skin, but hydration was no better than placebo and long-term use was needed. Gentler derivatives such as ascorbyl glucoside each have their own, smaller evidence base.
  • Vitamins C and E with ferulic acid. A manufacturer-funded study in nine adults found a 15% vitamin C, 1% vitamin E and 0.5% ferulic acid solution reduced UV-induced redness and DNA damage markers. That is short-term protection data, not proof of long-term anti-ageing.
  • Glutathione. A 2025 systematic review found only five clinical trials of topical glutathione and called for more randomised research.

Practical takeaway

  • Apply antioxidant serums in the morning under broad-spectrum sunscreen. They supplement sun protection and never replace it.
  • Choose L-ascorbic acid in opaque, airtight packaging, and replace it once it turns dark orange or brown.
  • Give tranexamic acid at least 8 to 12 weeks, and introduce one new active at a time.
  • See a dermatologist about pigmentation that persists, changes or returns. Melasma often relapses.

Key takeaway

Match the ingredient to the concern: tranexamic acid for melasma-type patches, vitamin C for tone and photoageing, supporting antioxidants for defence, and daily sunscreen underneath all of it.
